Edward Lorenz was solving differential equations for weather prediction in 1963. He entered initial conditions rounded to three decimal places instead of six. The trajectory diverged completely. A difference of one part in a thousand, amplified by iteration, produced an entirely different future.

The attractor is the shape the system traces forever — spiraling around one wing, then the other, folding near the center, never crossing itself, never repeating exactly. It is deterministic: given the exact same starting point, you get the exact same path. But the exact same starting point is never available. Every measurement has error. Every error, iterated, becomes the difference between prediction and chaos.

This is what numerical integration revealed — not just an equation solved, but a universe where the future is written but unreadable.
