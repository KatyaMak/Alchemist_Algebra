Prologue · Before the First Coordinate

Before there was a map, there was a direction. Before the direction, there was a need to know: which way? and how far?

The navigator on the open sea, the caravan leader reading stars above the Rub' al-Khali — these are not figures in a history of mathematics. They are the reason mathematics moved in this direction at all. The language they needed, the precise language, is what this manuscript contains. It is called Linear Algebra, which is a small name for an enormous capacity: to describe any direction in any space, to transform one arrangement of the world into another, and to find the line that lies most faithfully through the center of a scattered truth.

The Building Blocks
Drawing the Map · On Vectors, Matrices, and the Eigenspace
A vector is a direction held precisely — an arrow that carries both intention and magnitude. A matrix is not a table of numbers but a transformation: the world seen from a new angle without the world itself having moved. Where the Mudéjar ceiling extends from a single point into infinite pattern, a basis does the same — every reachable position in a space described by a small number of fundamental directions.

The eigenspace is the heart of this chapter's mystery. When a matrix acts on space — stretching it, rotating it, shearing it — most vectors are thrown off course. But certain special vectors hold their direction exactly, growing longer or shorter but never deflecting. These are the eigenvectors. The number that describes how much they scale is the eigenvalue. They are not an abstraction: they are the secret behind how a search engine finds what matters, and how a photograph survives compression without losing its essential truth.
vectors matrices eigenvalues & eigenvectors basis & span NumPy
Vectors
Before a vector was notation, it was a need. The caravan leader didn't say "a displacement of magnitude 40 in the northeast direction" — he said that way, that far. A vector is the precise form of that statement: direction and magnitude held together, inseparable.

In the plane, a vector is an arrow from the origin to a point. In higher dimensions — the ones data science actually lives in — it is a list of coordinates, each one a shadow cast onto an axis. The dot product is how two vectors ask each other a question: how much do we agree? Multiply the shadows, sum them up. When two vectors are perpendicular, the answer is zero — perfect silence between them. When they point the same way, the dot product reaches its maximum: the full product of their lengths. The boundary between these two states is Cauchy-Schwarz — nothing more than the geometry of shadows refusing to outrun the object that cast them.
import numpy as np  ·  a = np.array([3, 4])  ·  np.dot(a, b)  ·  np.linalg.norm(a)
In data science — every data point is a vector. Similarity between documents, users, predictions: measured by dot product and cosine angle.
Matrices
A matrix is not a container. It is a verb. It does something to space — stretches it, rotates it, collapses it, reflects it. When you multiply a matrix by a vector, you are not performing arithmetic. You are asking: where does this arrow land after the transformation?

This is the shift that changes everything. The grid of numbers is not the point. The point is what the grid enacts. A matrix that represents a rotation turns every vector in the plane by the same angle, with perfect consistency, without the plane itself knowing it moved. A matrix that projects flattens a three-dimensional world onto a surface — the way afternoon light casts the geometry of a room into shadows on the floor. Understanding a matrix means asking not "what are its entries" but what does it do to the space it acts on.
np.array([[1,2],[3,4]])  ·  A @ v  ·  np.dot(A, B)  ·  A.T  ·  np.linalg.det(A)
In data science — a dataset is a matrix. Each row a data point, each column a feature. Every operation on data — scaling, rotating, projecting — is a matrix transformation.
Eigenvalues & Eigenvectors
When a transformation moves through space — stretching some directions, compressing others — most vectors are thrown off their original course. They arrive somewhere new, pointing somewhere different. But certain vectors hold. They may grow longer or shorter, but they do not deflect. These are the eigenvectors: the directions the transformation cannot bend, only scale. The eigenvalue is how much it scales.

The collapse to zero is not failure. When an eigenvalue is zero, an entire dimension has been flattened to nothing — and that nothingness is precise information. It tells you where the space had no variance left to give, where the data carried no independent signal. A face is made of ten thousand pixel values, but its essential identity lives in a handful of eigenvectors. The Eigenfaces algorithm finds the directions that carry the most signal and keeps only those. What survives the collapse is not less than the original. It is more honest than the original.
np.linalg.eig(A)  →  (eigenvalues, eigenvectors)  ·  np.linalg.eigh(A) for symmetric matrices
In data science — PCA is eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix. Eigenvectors are the directions of maximum variance. Eigenvalues tell you how much of the signal each direction carries.
Basis & Span
A basis is the smallest set of directions from which an entire space can be described. Not every direction — just enough independent ones that any point can be reached by combining them in some proportion. The standard basis vectors point along the axes, one step each. But a basis can point anywhere, provided its vectors don't lie in the same direction as one another.

The span is the full territory those directions can reach together — combined freely, in any proportion. A basis spans its space completely, with nothing left over and nothing missing. This is why a Mudéjar ceiling pattern needs only a small number of generating shapes: combined with precision, they fill the entire wall. The pattern is not infinite because it has infinitely many shapes. It is infinite because a small number of shapes, combined freely, have infinite reach.
np.linalg.matrix_rank(A)  ·  np.linalg.solve(A, b)  ·  Linear independence tested via rank
In data science — features are basis vectors for a data space. Redundant features reduce effective rank. Dimensionality reduction finds a smaller basis that still spans what matters.
NumPy
NumPy is the workshop where linear algebra becomes computation. The ndarray holds vectors and matrices not as lists but as structures that know their own shape, and that carry mathematical operations built into their nature. You do not loop over entries and multiply by hand — you describe the operation at the level of the whole, and NumPy executes it efficiently across every element at once.

This is not merely convenient. It is a change in how you think. When you write A @ v, you are not asking Python to run a loop. You are stating: apply this transformation to this vector. The notation is closer to the mathematics than the arithmetic it replaces. NumPy is the bridge between the geometry you understand and the computation that acts on data at scale.
import numpy as np  ·  np.zeros() / np.ones() / np.eye(n)  ·  A.shape  ·  A.reshape()  ·  np.linalg.*
In data science — NumPy underlies pandas, scikit-learn, and every ML framework. Understanding array operations removes the abstraction layer between you and what the algorithms are doing.

The first arrival was geometric, not algebraic. Two formulas for the dot product — one using coordinates, one using lengths and angles — and the question of why they had to be equal. The algebraic version looked unbounded: just multiplication and addition, theoretically without limit. But the geometric version had cos θ in it, and cos θ is always trapped between −1 and 1. So the dot product can never exceed the product of the two lengths.

Then came the insight that made it land: coordinates are shadows. A vector's components are its projections onto the axes — and a shadow is always shorter than the object casting it. Always. The only time a shadow equals the object is when the vector points exactly along an axis. This is Cauchy-Schwarz — not a formula to memorize, but a fact the geometry refuses to violate. A famous name for something the territory had already made obvious.

The eigen-insight arrived through transformation. When a matrix stretches and rotates space, most vectors deflect. But certain vectors hold their line exactly, scaling without turning. The eigenvalue is how much they scale. And when an eigenvalue is zero — when a whole dimension collapses to nothing — that is not a failure. It is the space making a confession: there was no information here worth preserving. The Eigenfaces algorithm keeps only what survives that confession. A face reconstructed from its principal eigenvectors is not diminished. It is more honest than the original.

The Solver
Untangling the Knots · On Linear Systems and the Inversion of Matrices
Every system of equations is a set of constraints, each one a flat surface cutting through a space. Where they all intersect — if they intersect — is the answer. The question is how to find it without searching the entire space by hand.

Backsolving is the method of the patient craftsman: one equation at a time, working from the known toward the unknown. Inverting a matrix is the more ambitious move — to reverse an entire transformation, to undo the stretching and rotating all at once and recover what was there before. When the astronomer observed that light bends around mass, and worked backward from that bending to the mass that caused it, this is the mathematics they used: the inversion of a transformation to find its source.
systems of linear equations backsolving matrix inversion Gaussian elimination Pandas
Systems of Linear Equations
A single equation is a single constraint — one flat surface slicing through space. Every point on that surface satisfies the condition. Add a second equation and you have a second surface. The answer, if it exists, lives where all the surfaces intersect simultaneously.

This is not abstraction. The merchant calculating fair exchange across three currencies, the engineer balancing forces at a joint, the data scientist fitting a model with a dozen parameters — all of them are standing at the same intersection problem, asking the same question: where do all the constraints agree at once? Sometimes they agree at a single point. Sometimes along a whole line. Sometimes not at all — and that failure is itself information, a signal that the constraints are inconsistent, that the clues cannot all be true together.
np.linalg.solve(A, b)  ·  returns x such that A @ x = b  ·  raises LinAlgError if no unique solution
In data science — every regression model is a system of equations. Fitting a model means finding the parameter vector where all the constraints imposed by the data are simultaneously honored as well as possible.
Backsolving
Backsolving is the method of the patient craftsman. When a system has been reduced to triangular form — where the last equation has only one unknown, the second-to-last has two, and so on — you solve from the bottom up. One answer at a time, each one substituted back into the equation above it, until the whole system is resolved.

It is not elegant in the way inversion is elegant. It is disciplined. It respects the order in which information becomes available and uses each piece exactly when it is ready. The triangular structure is not a lucky accident — it is what Gaussian elimination is designed to produce. The patient work of reduction creates the conditions under which backsolving becomes effortless. Prepare the ground, then harvest.
scipy.linalg.solve_triangular(A, b)  ·  np.linalg.solve() uses this internally after LU decomposition
In data science — backsolving appears whenever a model has sequential structure: time series, hierarchical models, any system where earlier outputs feed into later equations.
Matrix Inversion
If a matrix is a verb — an action that stretches, rotates, or shears space — then its inverse is the undo. Apply the transformation, then apply its inverse, and you are back exactly where you started. The two verbs cancel each other completely.

But the undo button only works if the transformation preserved information. If the matrix collapsed a three-dimensional volume into a flat plane, the height dimension is gone permanently — you cannot recover what you never kept. This is why the determinant matters: it measures the factor by which the transformation scales volume. When the determinant is zero, volume has been annihilated. The space has been flattened to a lower dimension. No inverse exists, because there is no way to divide by zero and recover what was lost. The invertibility of a matrix is not a technical property. It is a statement about whether the verb remembered enough to be undone.
np.linalg.inv(A)  ·  np.linalg.det(A)  ·  prefer np.linalg.solve(A, b) over inv(A) @ b for numerical stability
In data science — singular matrices (det = 0) appear when features are perfectly correlated. The system has redundant information and cannot be uniquely solved. Regularization is the practical response: add a small value to make the matrix invertible again.
Gaussian Elimination
Gaussian elimination is the systematic reduction of a complicated system into one that can be solved by backsolving. Row by row, it eliminates variables — using one equation to cancel a term in another, working forward until the triangular form emerges and the path downward becomes clear.

Al-Khwarizmi's algebra was repair — the reunion of broken parts. Gaussian elimination is excavation: removing what obscures the answer until only the answer remains. It does not guess. It does not search. It reduces, methodically, until the structure of the solution is unavoidable. The algorithm is old enough to predate the name — Chinese mathematicians used equivalent methods in the first century. The form of the insight is ancient. The systematization is what modernity added.
np.linalg.solve(A, b)  ·  scipy.linalg.lu(A) for explicit LU factorization  ·  row operations via array slicing
In data science — Gaussian elimination underlies the numerical solvers in every ML library. When scikit-learn fits a linear model, it is performing elimination, not hand-checking each equation.
Pandas
Pandas is where the mathematics meets the data as it actually arrives — messy, labeled, heterogeneous, organized in rows and columns that mean something. The DataFrame is a matrix that remembers its own column names, its own index, its own context. It knows it is not just numbers. It knows what the numbers represent.

This matters because data science is not performed on abstract arrays. It is performed on measurements — of people, of prices, of temperatures, of words. Pandas holds the measurement alongside the number, so that when you solve the system and find the answer, you know what the answer refers to. NumPy computes. Pandas interprets. Together they close the gap between the equation and the world that generated it.
import pandas as pd  ·  df.values → numpy array  ·  pd.DataFrame(A)  ·  df.loc[], df.iloc[]  ·  df.apply()
In data science — Pandas is the entry point for almost every real dataset. Reading, cleaning, reshaping, and selecting data before it reaches the linear algebra layer that operates on it.

Chapter I was construction — learning what the objects are, what they do, how they act on space. Chapter II is inference. You are no longer building the coordinate system. You are standing at the finish line, looking at a transformed space, and tracing the path backward to find what caused it.

The inversion question arrived through the verb framing: if a matrix is an action, its inverse is the undo. But the undo only works if the action remembered enough. A transformation that collapses three dimensions into two has destroyed the height information permanently — there is no recovery. The determinant measures this: when it reaches zero, volume has been annihilated, a dimension has been lost, and the undo button breaks because you cannot divide by nothing to recover what no longer exists.

Gravitational lensing made it physical. Light from a distant star bends around a massive object and arrives at the telescope distorted into a ring. The astronomer sees the ring — the transformed output — and works backward through the known physics of gravity to find where the star actually is. This is matrix inversion as a real act. Not algebraic tidying. Detection. The mathematics stops being a set of rules and becomes a tool for discovery — the detective standing at the finish line, reading the clues the transformation left behind.

The Reality Check
Making a Best Guess · On Overdetermined Systems and the Line That Fits
In a perfect world, all the clues agree. In the real world, the data is noisy — the points scatter, the system is overdetermined, no single line passes through all of them. This is not a failure of mathematics. It is the condition mathematics was built to navigate.

Ordinary Least Squares regression is the answer to a geometric question: of all the lines possible, which one is least wrong? The matrix transpose is not a mechanical trick — it is the operation that makes this question answerable, that turns the overdetermined system into one that has a solution. The result is a line that carries the shape of the truth even when the truth arrives in pieces.
OLS regression matrix transpose best-fit line overdetermined systems Python visualization
OLS Regression
Ordinary Least Squares is the answer to a geometric question: of all possible lines through a cloud of points, which one is least wrong? Not perfectly right — that line doesn't exist when data is noisy. But least wrong: the one that minimizes the total squared distance between what it predicts and what is actually there.

The formula arrives as β = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy — and this is not an arbitrary expression. It is the closed-form solution to a calculus problem: minimize the sum of squared residuals. The transpose appears because we have more equations than unknowns, and XᵀX is the operation that makes the system square again — solvable, invertible, honest. OLS doesn't pretend the noise isn't there. It finds the line that stands in the best relationship to it.
beta = np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X) @ X.T @ y  ·  or: np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)
In data science — OLS is the foundation of linear regression. Every prediction model that outputs a continuous value is, at its core, finding the best-fit line through the scatter of what is true.
Matrix Transpose
The transpose flips a matrix across its main diagonal — rows become columns, columns become rows. The diagonal elements stay exactly where they are; the off-diagonal elements swap across it. It looks like a simple bookkeeping operation. It is not.

In OLS, the transpose is what makes an unsolvable system solvable. When your data matrix X has more rows than columns — more observations than parameters — the system Xβ = y is overdetermined: no single β satisfies every equation. Multiplying both sides by Xᵀ transforms the problem into XᵀXβ = Xᵀy, which is square and solvable. The transpose is the operation that turns the question "find β that satisfies everything" into "find β that is least wrong overall." And one elegant fact that travels with it: det(M) = det(Mᵀ) always. The determinant is blind to transposition.
A.T  ·  np.transpose(A)  ·  (A @ B).T == B.T @ A.T  ·  det(A) == det(A.T)
In data science — the transpose appears everywhere: covariance matrices, dot products between feature vectors, the normal equations of OLS. It is the quiet operation that makes the geometry of high-dimensional data tractable.
Best-Fit Line
The best-fit line is not the line that passes through the most points. It is the line that stands in the most honest relationship to all of them simultaneously — minimizing the sum of the squared vertical distances between each point and the line. Those distances are the residuals: what the model got wrong at each observation.

A line has two parameters: slope and intercept. OLS finds the specific slope and intercept that make the total squared residual as small as it can be. When you extend this to multiple features, the line becomes a hyperplane — same principle, higher dimension. And when the relationship is curved, you extend the feature matrix with polynomial terms: x², x³. The algebra is identical. The geometry is the same honest search through a space of more dimensions.
np.polyfit(x, y, deg=1)  ·  np.poly1d(coeffs)  ·  plt.plot(x, y_pred) for visualization
In data science — the best-fit line is the prediction. Its slope tells you how much the output changes per unit of input. Its intercept tells you where it starts. Residuals tell you where the model is wrong and how systematically.
Overdetermined Systems
A system of equations is overdetermined when there are more equations than unknowns — more constraints than degrees of freedom. In a perfect world, the extra constraints would be consistent and redundant. In the real world, they contradict each other: no single solution satisfies all of them exactly.

This is not a pathological case. It is the normal condition of real data. Every dataset with more observations than parameters is an overdetermined system. The question is not "how do we find the exact solution" — there isn't one. The question is "how do we find the best approximate solution": the parameter vector that violates the constraints as little as possible, in aggregate. OLS is the answer. The overdetermined system is not a problem to eliminate. It is the honest description of what data is.
np.linalg.lstsq(A, b, rcond=None)  ·  returns least-squares solution when system has no exact answer
In data science — every real regression problem is overdetermined. You have hundreds or thousands of observations and a handful of parameters. The model is always an approximation. The question is only how good an approximation.
Python Visualization
Matplotlib is how the invisible becomes visible. The scatter of points, the best-fit line running through them, the residuals as vertical drops between each point and the line — none of this can be held in the mind in its full dimensionality without being drawn. Visualization is not decoration. It is verification: the moment you check whether the mathematics you applied matches the reality it was supposed to describe.

The first time you plot a regression line over the data that produced it, something confirms. The line doesn't pass through every point — it was never supposed to. But it runs through the center of the scatter with a quiet authority, and you can see at a glance whether it is capturing the signal or missing it entirely. This is the moment algebra becomes observation.
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt  ·  plt.scatter(x, y)  ·  plt.plot(x, y_pred, color='gold')  ·  plt.show()
In data science — visualization is how you catch what the numbers alone won't tell you. Heteroscedasticity, outliers, nonlinearity — these show up in plots before they show up in statistics.
