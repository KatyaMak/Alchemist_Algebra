Prologue · On the Nature of This Manuscript

Everything that lives, changes. Everything that changes, has a rate. Everything that has a rate, has a derivative.

The first mathematicians who asked about motion found themselves confronted with a paradox: to know the speed of something at a single instant, you must compare two positions — but two positions require two moments, and a single instant has only one. Zeno saw this and declared motion impossible. Newton saw it and invented calculus.

The answer was not to ignore the paradox but to approach it without touching it. Not to reach the single instant — but to ask: what does the measurement approach, as the two moments draw infinitely close together? The limit. The destination that the calculation is always traveling toward, even if it never quite arrives.

Expected Values and the Normal Distribution
What reality tends toward, when chance runs many times
Not what will happen — but the center of gravity of all possible happenings, weighted by their likelihood. A single experiment produces an outcome. Many experiments produce a distribution. And when you average across enough samples drawn from almost any distribution, something remarkable occurs: those averages arrange themselves into the same familiar shape, every time, regardless of what generated them.

This is the architecture beneath statistics. Not a collection of formulas but a single deep fact about what averaging does to randomness at scale — and why the bell curve appears everywhere it does.

Expected Value
Not a prediction of any single outcome. The center of gravity of all possible outcomes, each weighted by the probability that it occurs. Roll a fair die enough times and the average settles toward 3.5 — which no single roll ever produces. 3.5 is not a result. It is the structure the results orbit.

Expected value is the bridge between probability and measurement. It asks: if the experiment ran forever, where would the average land? The answer is not uncertain — it is mathematically determined by the distribution itself. This is why actuaries can price risk without knowing which house will burn, why casinos profit without knowing which player will win. The individual outcome is unpredictable. The long-run average is exact.
E[X] = Σ x·P(x)  ·  np.mean(samples)  ·  np.average(values, weights=probs)
In data science — expected value underlies loss functions, model evaluation, and Bayesian updating. The mean of a distribution is its expected value. Every prediction a model makes is an implicit claim about expected value.
The Central Limit Theorem
The most astonishing theorem in all of statistics. Take any random variable — dice, wages, heights, anything. Sample from it repeatedly and calculate the sample mean each time. As the number of samples grows, those means will arrange themselves into a normal distribution — regardless of the shape of the original variable.

Not approximately. Exactly, in the limit. The original distribution can be skewed, bimodal, uniform, wildly irregular — it does not matter. The means converge to the bell curve. This is why the normal distribution appears everywhere in nature: not because the world is normally distributed, but because averages always are. The theorem doesn't describe the world. It describes what happens to information when you aggregate it.
sample_means = [np.mean(np.random.choice(population, size=n)) for _ in range(1000)]  ·  plt.hist(sample_means)
In data science — the CLT justifies using normal-distribution assumptions for hypothesis testing even when data isn't normal, provided sample sizes are large enough. It is the theoretical foundation beneath much of frequentist statistics.
Normal Distribution
What emerges when many small, independent forces push in many directions and no single force dominates. The bell curve. Symmetrical, with most mass at the center and thin tails at the extremes. Two parameters determine everything: the mean marks the center, the standard deviation measures the spread.

Probabilities correspond to areas under this curve — which is exactly where calculus enters the story. The area beneath a continuous distribution requires integration, integration requires derivatives to understand, and so the first and second chapters of this manuscript are not separate subjects. They are the same question approached from two directions. Statistics asks what the shape is. Calculus asks how to measure what's under it.
scipy.stats.norm(loc=μ, scale=σ)  ·  .pdf(x) .cdf(x) .ppf(q)  ·  np.random.normal(μ, σ, size=n)
In data science — normality assumptions underlie linear regression, many hypothesis tests, and confidence intervals. Knowing when data is normal (and when it isn't) determines which tools are valid.
Variance & Standard Deviation
The mean tells you where the distribution is centered. Variance tells you how far it spreads from that center. It is the average of the squared distances from the mean — squared to eliminate the sign, so that deviations above and below the center don't cancel each other out.

Standard deviation is the square root of variance: it restores the units to those of the original data. A distribution with small standard deviation is tightly clustered around its mean. A large standard deviation means the outcomes are widely scattered. The bell curve's width is entirely determined by its standard deviation. Change the mean and the curve slides left or right. Change the standard deviation and it narrows or broadens. These two numbers contain the whole shape.
np.var(data)  ·  np.std(data)  ·  ddof=1 for sample (unbiased) vs population variance
In data science — standard deviation is the fundamental measure of spread. Feature scaling (standardization) divides by standard deviation. Outliers are defined relative to it. Confidence intervals are built from it.
NumPy & SciPy
NumPy provides the numerical foundations — arrays, random sampling, descriptive statistics. SciPy extends into the territory of distributions: not just generating samples but computing probabilities, cumulative areas, quantiles, and performing statistical tests.

The division is intuitive: NumPy handles the arithmetic of arrays, SciPy handles the mathematics of probability. Together they close the gap between a distribution defined in theory and a distribution computed from data. And the autograder that rejects correct code because the import is in the wrong place — that too is a lesson. Mathematical truth and implementation constraint are different things. Both are real. Both must be satisfied.
import numpy as np  ·  from scipy import stats  ·  stats.norm.cdf(x)  ·  stats.ttest_ind(a, b)
In data science — SciPy's stats module is the standard library for probability distributions and hypothesis testing. Understanding what it computes, not just how to call it, is the difference between using statistics and understanding it.

Derivatives
The Language of Instantaneous Change · The rate at which the world is changing, right now
To know the speed of something at a single instant, you must compare two positions — but two positions require two moments, and a single instant has only one. The resolution: never reach the point. Instead, ask what the measurement approaches as the two moments draw infinitely close together. The limit. Not an approximation — the exact value of the destination the calculation is always traveling toward.

From this single question, the entire architecture of calculus unfolds: power rule, chain rule, gradient descent. And at the end of the chain, backpropagation — the algorithm that made the video that started everything possible.
The Pilgrimage of H · Written April 2026

There was once a traveler who wanted to know the speed of a river at a single point — not upstream, not downstream, but here, at this exact stone, at this exact moment.

He did what anyone would do. He dropped two sticks into the water — one here, one a little downstream — and measured how long the second stick took to reach the first. Distance divided by time. A number arrived. He wrote it down.

But the number troubled him. Because the river between the sticks was not the river at the stone. It was the river between. He had measured a stretch when he wanted a moment.

So he moved the sticks closer together. The number changed slightly. Closer still. It changed again, less this time. He kept going — closer, closer — watching the number settle, like sediment finding the bottom of still water.

He never placed the sticks in the same spot. He knew he couldn't. A distance of zero, a time of zero — that way lies division by nothing, which is the one door mathematics will not open.

But he saw where the number was going. He saw its destination, even though it never arrived.

That destination — the value the number eternally approached without touching — was the speed of the river at the stone. Exact. True. Unreachable by measurement, but knowable by understanding.

He had not measured it. He had seen it.

That is what calculus is. Not a technique. A change in what you are able to see.

Limits
The derivative cannot be calculated at a single point directly — to measure change you need two positions, but a single instant has only one. The resolution: never reach the point. Ask what the measurement approaches as the interval shrinks toward zero.

This is not approximation. It is the exact value of the destination the calculation is always traveling toward. The limit exists when the expression approaches the same value from both sides. When it does, the derivative exists. When it does not — the function has a corner, a jump, a discontinuity — the derivative fails at that point. The tool works best when the function is smooth and continuous. Zeno saw the paradox and concluded motion was impossible. The limit sees the same paradox and concludes: we don't need to arrive. We need to see where we're going.
from sympy import limit, symbols, oo  ·  limit(expr, x, 0)  ·  numerical: (f(x+h) - f(x)) / h as h → 0
In data science — limits underlie the definition of every derivative used in optimization. Understanding them means understanding why gradient descent works, and why it sometimes doesn't.
Power Rule
The derivative of xⁿ is n·xⁿ⁻¹. Not a rule handed down from authority — what survives when the limit process is applied to a polynomial term using the binomial theorem. Expand (x+h)ⁿ. Subtract xⁿ. Divide by h. Let h approach zero. Most terms still contain h and vanish. The only survivor is n·xⁿ⁻¹ — the only term where h cancels completely.

The power drops by one with each differentiation. Distance is cubic → speed is squared → acceleration is linear → jerk is constant → the next derivative is zero. This is not coincidence. It is what must happen when you ask "how fast is this changing" of a polynomial, recursively. In Russian: производные — produced things. The derivative is not a reduction of the function. It is what the function generates at each moment.
from sympy import diff, symbols  ·  x = symbols('x')  ·  diff(x**3 + 2*x, x)  →  3x² + 2
In data science — the power rule is applied in every polynomial feature, every activation function derivative, every gradient computation in a neural network layer.
Chain Rule
When one function sits inside another, the chain rule finds the derivative of the whole by multiplying the individual sensitivities. The derivative of f(g(x)) is f′(g(x)) · g′(x). The outer function's rate of change, times the inner function's rate of change. Named for its structure: the functions are linked in a chain, each link's sensitivity multiplying the next.

The chain rule is not an advanced topic. It is the engine underneath every neural network ever trained. Backpropagation — the algorithm that makes gradient descent computable through many layers — is the chain rule applied recursively, backward through each layer in sequence. Karpathy built this from scratch. The derivative explorer artifact below shows the same mathematics at smaller scale. The chain is the same chain. The links are just more numerous.
diff(f(g(x)), x) = diff(f, g) * diff(g, x)  ·  sympy handles this automatically via diff()
In data science — the chain rule is backpropagation. Every weight update in every neural network is the chain rule applied through the computational graph, layer by layer, back to the input.
Gradient Descent
A loss function measures how wrong a model's predictions are. To make the model less wrong, move in the direction that reduces the loss — downhill on the loss surface. The gradient tells you which direction is downhill: it is a vector of partial derivatives, one for each parameter, each one measuring the slope in that parameter's direction.

The update rule: θ_new = θ − α · ∇L. Subtract a fraction of the gradient from the current position. Repeat. The learning rate α controls the step size. Too large — the step overshoots the minimum and the loss grows. Too small — convergence is very slow. The orange dot in the gradient descent artifact finds the bottom when the gradient approaches zero: the stationary point, where the slope is flat, where there is nothing left to descend toward. This is what Karpathy built. This is the video that started everything.
theta = theta - learning_rate * gradient  ·  for each step until np.linalg.norm(gradient) < tolerance
In data science — gradient descent is the universal optimization algorithm. Every ML model that learns from data is performing some variant of it: batch, stochastic, or mini-batch.
Partial Derivatives
When a function depends on multiple variables, the partial derivative asks: if I change just this one variable, holding everything else frozen — how does the output change? The other variables become constants. Constants vanish under the power rule or multiply through unchanged.

For f(x,y) = x²y + y³: the partial with respect to x treats y as frozen — result: 2xy. The partial with respect to y treats x as frozen — result: x² + 3y². In a neural network with millions of parameters, there are millions of partial derivatives — one per weight. Together they form the gradient vector. Gradient descent follows all of them simultaneously. This was the first derivative calculated in one's own head, before reading the answer. April 2026. Ten lectures done.
from sympy import diff  ·  diff(f, x) for ∂f/∂x  ·  diff(f, y) for ∂f/∂y  ·  gradient = [∂f/∂x, ∂f/∂y]
In data science — the gradient of the loss function is the vector of all partial derivatives with respect to model parameters. Computing it efficiently (via backpropagation) is what makes deep learning trainable.
SymPy
SymPy is symbolic mathematics in Python — the difference between computing a number and computing a form. Where NumPy evaluates expressions at specific values, SymPy manipulates the expressions themselves: differentiating, integrating, simplifying, factoring, solving symbolically.

diff(x**3 + 2*x, x) returns 3*x**2 + 2 — not a number but the derivative as a mathematical object, ready to be evaluated anywhere or manipulated further. This is how you verify the power rule, explore the chain rule, compute partial derivatives without choosing specific values first. SymPy is the Alchemist's workbench for calculus: the place where the form of the mathematics is held and examined before the numbers arrive.
from sympy import symbols, diff, integrate, limit, simplify  ·  x = symbols('x')  ·  expr.subs(x, 2)
In data science — SymPy is used for deriving gradient expressions, verifying automatic differentiation, and understanding the mathematical structure of algorithms before implementing them numerically.

Integrals
The Mathematics of Accumulation · On area, summation, and the fundamental theorem
The derivative asks what is happening at this exact instant. The integral asks what has accumulated across all the instants together. They are not separate tools — they are the same question asked from opposite directions. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus is the statement of this symmetry: differentiation and integration are inverse operations, each undoing what the other does.

Area under a curve. Total probability across a distribution. Cumulative change over time. These are all integrals — and the normal distribution, which appeared in Chapter I as a shape, becomes in Chapter III a question of area: what fraction of outcomes fall within one standard deviation? Two? The bell curve is not just a shape. It is a function to be integrated. And at the edge of integration lives chaos — where a simple deterministic rule, iterated, produces futures that cannot be read.
Riemann sums definite integrals fundamental theorem PDF & CDF chaos & butterfly effect
Riemann Sums
The traveler wanted to know the area beneath a curve — the total accumulation of something varying continuously. He did what anyone would do: he sliced the region into rectangles, computed each rectangle's area, and added them up. The rectangles were wrong. They were either too tall or too short at every point.

So he made them thinner. The total improved. Thinner still — better again. He watched the sum settle, like sediment finding the bottom of still water. He never reached the curve exactly. But he saw where the sum was going — the value it approached without ever quite touching. That limit is the integral. Not an approximation of the area. The area itself, defined as what the approximation eternally approaches.
scipy.integrate.quad(f, a, b)  ·  np.trapz(y, x)  ·  Riemann manually: sum(f(x)*dx for x in partition)
In data science — numerical integration appears whenever a continuous probability distribution is evaluated. The probability that a value falls in an interval is the integral of the PDF over that interval.
Definite Integrals
A definite integral has two bounds: a starting point and an ending point. It asks how much has accumulated between them. The result is a single number — not a function, not a shape, but a quantity. The area between x=0 and x=3 under the curve y=x². The total probability between one and two standard deviations from the mean.

The bounds matter precisely. Change them and the answer changes. The integral from a to b is not the same as the integral from b to a — it is its negative, because accumulation has a direction. This signed area is not a complication. It is the full picture of what the function is doing — growing here, shrinking there, the positive and negative contributions tallied honestly together.
scipy.integrate.quad(f, a, b)  ·  returns (value, error_estimate)  ·  scipy.integrate.dblquad for double integrals
In data science — the CDF is a definite integral of the PDF from −∞ to x. Every probability calculation from a continuous distribution is a definite integral, computed numerically by the statistics library.
The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
Two operations that look completely unrelated — one adds up infinitely many infinitely thin slices, one measures the slope at a single point — are inverse operations of each other. Integrate a function, then differentiate the result: you recover the original function. Differentiate, then integrate: same. They undo each other completely.

This is not a coincidence to be verified by calculation. It is the deep symmetry that makes calculus one subject rather than two subjects with similar names. The rate of accumulation at any point equals the function's value at that point. How fast area is being added at x is exactly f(x). The theorem doesn't connect differentiation and integration — it reveals that they were always the same operation, seen from opposite sides of the same question.
from sympy import integrate, diff, symbols  ·  x = symbols('x')  ·  diff(integrate(f, x), x) == f  ·  always true
In data science — the FTC is why the PDF and CDF are derivatives and integrals of each other. It is also why gradient descent works: the gradient is the derivative of the loss, and following it is accumulating steps toward the minimum.
PDF & CDF
The PDF — Probability Density Function — describes how dense the probability is at each point. The bell curve is a PDF. It answers: how concentrated is the probability right here at x?

The CDF — Cumulative Distribution Function — describes how much probability has accumulated up to x. It starts at 0, ends at 1, always increases. It answers: what fraction of outcomes fall at or below x?

Snow accumulating on the ground. The total depth at time x is the CDF. The rate of snowfall at moment x is the PDF. Integrate the snowfall rate and you get the total depth. Differentiate the total depth and you get back the snowfall rate. The Fundamental Theorem, made visible in weather. These are not two different descriptions of a distribution. They are the same distribution, seen from accumulation and from rate simultaneously.
scipy.stats.norm.pdf(x)  ·  scipy.stats.norm.cdf(x)  ·  cdf is integral of pdf  ·  pdf = derivative of cdf
In data science — the CDF is used for percentiles, p-values, and confidence intervals. P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = CDF(b) − CDF(a). Every hypothesis test is asking a question about area under a distribution.
Chaos & the Butterfly Effect
Edward Lorenz was solving differential equations for weather prediction. He entered initial conditions rounded to three decimal places instead of six. The trajectory diverged completely. A difference of one part in a thousand, amplified by iteration, produced an entirely different future.

The logistic map xₙ₊₁ = r·xₙ·(1−xₙ) shows the same structure at smaller scale. At low r, it settles to one stable value. As r grows, it bifurcates — oscillating between two values, then four, then eight. The doublings accelerate, then collapse into chaos. But inside the chaos, pockets of order appear — islands of period-3, period-5 cycles, then chaos again. The system is deterministic: given the exact starting point, you get the exact same path. But the exact starting point is never available. Every measurement has error. Every error, iterated, becomes the difference between prediction and the unreadable future.
def logistic(r, x): return r * x * (1 - x)  ·  scipy.integrate.solve_ivp() for Lorenz ODEs  ·  small dt matters
In data science — chaos is why long-range prediction fails in complex systems. It is also why numerical integration step size matters: too large and the solver diverges from the true trajectory, amplifying error with every step.
